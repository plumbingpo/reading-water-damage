No part of restoring a water-damaged building matters more than structural drying, yet it is also where untrained crews make the most mistakes. Once standing water has been extracted, a great deal of moisture is still held inside the structure: in insulation, concrete, subfloors, wood framing, drywall and other components. Controlled evaporation and dehumidification, applied scientifically, are needed to get that moisture out before a wet structure develops mold, rotting wood, degraded materials and odors.

The structural drying technicians at Reading Restoration are IICRC-certified and trained in psychrometry, the science of how temperature, humidity and moisture in air relate. Using this knowledge, they calculate the specific equipment placement, temperature settings and airflow patterns needed to create the right evaporation conditions for each drying situation. Plaster, concrete, hardwood and drywall each dry at their own rate, so each needs its own approach to get down to an acceptable moisture level with no added damage.

Daily monitoring is built into how we dry a structure, and it relies on calibrated meters, thermal cameras and thermo-hygrometers. We document moisture readings throughout the drying process, creating a detailed drying log that shows progressive moisture reduction in all affected materials. Those readings guide daily equipment adjustments that keep the drying as efficient as it can be. Drying is complete only when all affected materials reach acceptable moisture standards as defined by IICRC S500 guidelines, not when surfaces simply feel dry to the touch.

Structural Drying Prevention Tips

Taking preventive measures can help you avoid costly water damage issues down the road. Follow these tips from our IICRC-certified technicians:

  • Do not place personal items on top of drying equipment or block the airflow from air movers
  • The sooner drying starts after water gets in, the better things turn out, so do not wait
  • Until a technician removes the equipment, keep it running day and night without breaks
  • Trust the moisture meter readings, because surfaces can feel dry while moisture remains deep in materials
  • Every hour of run time matters, so resist switching the machines off for some quiet
  • Each unit is positioned according to the moisture map, so ask before shifting anything

Why Professional Structural Drying Matters

Structural drying is a science, not a guessing game. Simply placing fans and hoping for the best is the most common cause of secondary mold damage after water events. To do it professionally, you have to know how each material takes on and gives up moisture, work out the grain depression, meaning the moisture removal capacity, for the job, and check progress every day. Without this knowledge, under-drying leaves moisture that feeds mold, while over-drying can damage wood and other materials.

The equipment used for professional structural drying is fundamentally different from consumer products. Commercial LGR (low-grain refrigerant) dehumidifiers remove 10 to 20 times more moisture from the air than residential dehumidifiers, and high-velocity air movers create the precise airflow patterns needed to accelerate evaporation from structural surfaces. Between the investment in equipment and the training needed to run it, it is no surprise that DIY drying falls well short of a professional job.

When to Call for Structural Drying in Reading

Recognizing the warning signs early can save you time, money, and prevent significant damage to your property. Watch for these telltale signs that professional help is needed:

  • Wood flooring with boards that cup, crown or pull apart
  • Materials breaking down in a spot that got wet before and was not dried by professionals
  • After water damage, a meter shows elevated moisture in the floor, wall or ceiling
  • Trapped moisture often announces itself with a persistent musty odor after cleanup
  • Window glass that fogs up near the affected rooms
  • Rooms that stay humid even with windows open and fans running
  • Baseboards or door trim that swell, warp or pull away from the wall
